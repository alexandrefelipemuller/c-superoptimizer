\title{How Much Is the Search and How Much Is the Bias? Hand-Designed Generators in Non-Learning Program Synthesis}

\author{Alexandre Felipe Muller de Souza}
\affiliation{%
  \position{M.Sc.}

  \state{PR}

\email{alexandrefelipemuller@gmail.com}

\begin{abstract}
We ask how much of the success of a program-synthesis search with no machine learning is due to the search and how much to hand-designed
generators. The system is a beam search over the syntax trees of a small imperative language, driven by generators of loop and conditional shapes and
by an exact count of solved training cases. On the seven integer problems of PSB2, with 100 fresh seeds, a 900\,s budget and 2,000 unseen test cases per run,
it solves GCD, Basement, Find Pair and Paired Digits in every run, Fuel Cost and Luhn in 91 and 90, and Coin Sums in 14, above the published PushGP counts on all seven (a historical reference, not a controlled comparison: the baseline was not rerun). Turning off six generator pieces that we
designed after reading the failures on these same problems drops the total from 595 to 113 of 700 runs. A control that keeps the generators and removes all
selection feedback (random search) solves as many runs as the beam (187 against 183 of 210), so on these problems the result belongs to the generators and
the selection adds nothing measurable. On 17 further problems we detect no effect of the pieces, but most are at floor or ceiling in both arms and the test can see only
large effects. A held-out set frozen before implementation (PSB2 Square Digits, Bowling, Camel Case and PSB1 Median, 30 seeds) gives the same picture, though every problem is at floor or ceiling: the pieces make no difference
(Median is solved in 30 of 30 runs with or without them; the other three in none), and random search finds nothing on Median. One narrow piece, a counting test on residues, does help within its own family (0 of 80 to 63 of 80 runs). Predictions were registered before the runs, and the failed ones are reported.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{program synthesis, beam search, genetic programming, PSB2, benchmark, negative results}

\section{Introduction}

Writing a program from a handful of examples is a task people do every day, and teaching machines to do it is one of the oldest goals
of artificial intelligence. Today the attention is on large neural models that produce code directly, which makes it easy to forget that
program synthesis from input--output examples asks for something different from approximating a function: the result is a program that can
be read, tested outside the examples, and reasoned about, and a search that finds it need not learn anything from other programs. Besides the neural models, the field has two long-standing non-neural
lines, genetic programming (GP) and enumerative or constraint-based synthesis. These have a
shared yardstick in the General Program Synthesis Benchmark Suite and its successor PSB2~\cite{helmuth2015psb1,helmuth2021psb2},
whose protocol (200 training cases, 2,000 unseen test cases, a budget of 60 million program executions, 100 runs per problem) makes
success rates comparable across methods. The published baseline there is PushGP with lexicase selection, which solves 13 of the 25
PSB2 problems at least once and several of the easiest ones only in a minority of runs~\cite{helmuth2021psb2}.

This paper reports a program-synthesis system built to see how far a search with \emph{no learning} can go on that yardstick, and,
as importantly, how much of what it achieves is due to the search itself and how much to pieces that its author designed after
looking at where it failed. The system is a beam search over a small imperative language (integer variables, an array, loops,
conditionals, swaps, bounded recursion) in which candidate programs come from a mix of mutation, fresh generation and a small
set of generic generators (a ``combinator'' of loop shapes and relations, a scalar-loop generator, enumeration of short tails),
with selection by the exact number of solved cases. There is no neural model, no learned prior and no language model; there are, however, hand-built biases, and separating their effect from the search's is the point of the paper.

The paper asks four questions. \textbf{RQ1}: what success rate does the system reach on the integer subset of PSB2 under the PSB2 criterion? \textbf{RQ2}: how much of it is due to the hand-designed generator pieces, and how much to the beam's selection? \textbf{RQ3}: do those pieces help on problems that did not guide their design (17 earlier problems and a frozen held-out set of four)? \textbf{RQ4}: how does the result depend on the computational budget? Section~\ref{sec:psb2} answers RQ1 and RQ4, Section~\ref{sec:ablation} RQ2, and Sections~\ref{sec:transfer}, \ref{sec:heldout} and~\ref{sec:narrow} RQ3. It is an experimental study of a non-learning imperative search, not a proposal of a new search algorithm.

We emphasise method as much as numbers. Every comparison between two of our own configurations is run with matched random seeds. Predictions are written down before
the runs and failed predictions are kept in the record. A change becomes a default only if it passes a regression battery of 77
suite problems with a no-drop-of-two rule. The full log (146 numbered sections) is part of the artefact, in the public repository at \url{https://github.com/alexandrefelipemuller/AlgoSearch}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item On the integer subset of PSB2 (seven of the 25 problems) and with 100 fresh seeds, the system solves four problems in all
        100 runs, two (Fuel Cost, Luhn) in about 90, and one (Coin Sums) in 14. Under the same protocol the published PushGP baseline
        solves GCD, Fuel Cost, Basement, Find Pair, Luhn, Paired Digits and Coin Sums in 8, 50, 1, 4, 0, 8 and 2 of 100 runs
        (Section~\ref{sec:psb2}). The budget matters: with twice the time Luhn rises from 90 to 98 runs and Fuel Cost from 91 to 94, and no solved run exceeds the 60 million executions.
  \item An ablation showing where the gain comes from: six generator pieces, each cheap and each checked for cost on the 77-problem suite
        (a check with limited sensitivity), take the seven problems from 34 to 178 solved runs out of 210 on the seeds they were developed on and, out of sample, from 113 to 595 of 700 (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
  \item A random-search control: with the generators fixed and all selection feedback removed, 187 of 210 runs are solved against 183 for the beam, so the generators, not the selection, carry the result on these problems (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
  \item A transfer result: the pieces were designed on those seven problems, and
        on 17 problems they were not designed for (PSB2 and PSB1-style) we detect no effect, with power only for large effects (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). One narrow, generic form
        (counting items by a remainder test) does close its own family, from 0 to 63 of 80 runs, at no measured cost
        (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}).
  \item A frozen held-out test of four problems (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}), with predictions registered before the problems were implemented: the pieces make no difference, but one problem is solved in every run without them and three are never solved, so the test cannot see moderate effects; random search fails where no piece applies.
  \item A documented protocol and a log of every experiment (the raw run output is not committed; see Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), including the claims that did not survive (for instance, ``six of
        seven problems above 93\%'' was true at 30 seeds and false at 100).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.}
Only seven of the 25 PSB2 problems are expressible in our language, the integer and vector-of-integer ones; the rest need floating
point or free-form strings. We do not claim to cover PSB2 (Middle Character, for example, is solved by PushGP in 57 of 100 runs and by our system in at most 2 of 20 with our own encoding). Section~\ref{sec:discussion} lists the limitations.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} places the work among GP, enumerative and learned
synthesis. Section~\ref{sec:search} describes the search, Section~\ref{sec:protocol} the protocol, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results,
and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} the limitations.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Genetic programming and benchmark suites.}
Push is a stack-based language designed so that evolved programs are always syntactically valid, and PushGP evolves such programs
with a population-based search~\cite{spector2002push}. The General Program Synthesis Benchmark Suite (PSB1) collected 29 problems from
introductory programming sources and gave PushGP results as a reference~\cite{helmuth2015psb1}; PSB2 added 25 harder problems taken
from programming katas and courses, again with a PushGP baseline~\cite{helmuth2021psb2}. Our experiments use the PSB2 protocol and its
data files for the integer subset, and the published PushGP rates as the reference point. We differ in kind, not only in
details: our programs are written in an imperative language with explicit loops and the search is a beam search over generated
candidates, not an evolving population.

\paragraph{Selection, generalisation and novelty.}
Lexicase selection, which filters the population case by case in random order, is the standard parent-selection method for PushGP
on these suites~\cite{helmuth2015lexicase,helmuth2021psb2}. It also has known weaknesses: on problems with few distinct outputs, such as Boolean ones,
it overfits the training cases, and generalisation can improve when the run continues after the first program that fits the training
set~\cite{sobania2021generalizability}. Our protocol keeps a post-solution window and an
independent adversarial check for the same reason. Combining lexicase selection with novelty search was studied by Jundt and
Helmuth~\cite{jundt2019novelty}, who found that novelty search alone performed poorly on program synthesis and combined it with lexicase selection. A different kind of
novelty term in our system, over the running-time profile of a program rather than its per-case errors, gave a mixed result on one
problem pair and eight seeds: it shrank the archive of solutions about a hundredfold and found heap sort in 8 of 8 runs (5 of 8 in
the control), but discovered no new family of algorithms, and it remains an opt-in flag.

\paragraph{Search for efficient algorithms and recursion.}
GP has been used to evolve list-search algorithms, including logarithmic-time search, by Wolfson and Sipper, who generalise the representation
with automatically defined functions so that the result is not biased towards binary search~\cite{wolfson2010efficient}. This is prior art for
our own experiments on searching for sub-linear algorithms (not reported here). In those, an opt-in piece that halves an interval let the search
assemble a known logarithmic-time peak search in 9 of 10 runs and a membership search in 5 of 10, failed on a third problem (the minimum
of a rotated sorted array), and is not part of the default configuration; we do not present it as novel.

\paragraph{Enumerative, syntax-guided and sketch-based synthesis.}
Syntax-guided synthesis casts the problem as finding an expression, from a grammar, that meets a specification~\cite{alur2013sygus}, and
enumerative solvers explore that grammar bottom-up, often with equivalence pruning~\cite{gulwani2017program}. Recursive programs can be
synthesised from examples by an enumerative method as well~\cite{albarghouthi2013escher}. These systems mostly work on loop-free
expressions or on a specification given as a formula, while ours searches imperative programs with loops from examples only. Our
short-tail enumeration is a small instance of the same idea: after a block of generated code, the remaining few statements are enumerated.
Stochastic superoptimisation searches over instruction sequences with a cost function~\cite{schkufza2013stoke}; our selection uses
solved-case counts, not a cost model.

\paragraph{Library learning and learned guidance.}
DreamCoder and Stitch learn a library of reusable functions from solved tasks and use it to guide search~\cite{ellis2020dreamcoder,bowers2023stitch}.
Our generic pieces play a similar role, a vocabulary of useful loop shapes, but they are \emph{designed by hand}, not learned, which is both the
reason they transfer poorly to unseen problems and the reason their effect can be isolated and measured.

\paragraph{Language-model-based synthesis.}
Language models trained on code solve many programming problems directly~\cite{chen2021codex}. We do not compare with them: the goal here is a
non-neural baseline with an inspectable search, and the comparison that matters for the claims in this paper is against GP on the same protocol.

\paragraph{Position.}
Our system is not new as a search method, and our control shows that its selection is not what carries the result on the benchmark subset; its relation to the above is that it applies hand-built generators of imperative programs to a
shared benchmark, measures how much they are worth, reports the failures and negative transfer alongside the successes, and publishes the whole log. The closest results are the
published PushGP rates on PSB2~\cite{helmuth2021psb2}.

\section{The Search}
\label{sec:search}

\subsection{Language and Interpreter}
Programs are written in a small imperative language, Mini-C: integer scalar variables (one of them, \texttt{v0}, holds the length of
the input), one input array \texttt{arr} that also receives the output, a scratch array \texttt{aux}, \texttt{if}, \texttt{while},
\texttt{break}, \texttt{swap}, a bounded recursive call over an interval, comparison, arithmetic (\texttt{+ - * / \%}) and bitwise operators.
A tree-walking interpreter runs a candidate on an input and returns the final array; a step limit turns a non-terminating loop into a
timeout. Programs are abstract syntax trees, so the search can grow, mutate and recombine them without a syntactic validity problem.

\subsection{Beam Search and Selection}
A problem supplies instances at a difficulty level and a predicate that decides whether an output is correct. The search keeps the
\texttt{beamWidth} best programs at the current size and builds the next generation from three sources: mutations of the beam,
freshly generated programs, and programs seeded from a small library of skeletons (Section~\ref{sec:skeletons}). Candidates are ranked by the
number of training cases solved exactly, with a partial score as a tie-break. Each size step works as follows. Every beam survivor is kept unchanged and also grown or mutated into a variant, so that the best program is never lost to a bad mutation (elitism); the top survivors also get the best enumerated tail. The remaining slots, up to \texttt{candidates}, are fresh random programs of the target size, de-duplicated against earlier fresh programs by a canonical text that ignores variable names and dead code; survivors are always rescored and are not de-duplicated. Ranking is by solved cases, then by the partial score, then by smaller size, and the top \texttt{beamWidth} survive. No diversity mechanism is active in the PSB2 runs: the optional immigrant, shape-niche, behaviour-niche and crossover rates exist in the code and are zero in the run scripts. One execution is counted as one program on one case, so a step with 1,500 fresh and 1,000 grown candidates on 200 cases costs 500,000 executions. A program that solves every case becomes a
solution; unless the run is a single-level benchmark run (as in PSB2), it must keep solving further levels of larger instances before it is
confirmed. Two further guards reduce false positives: a regression guard adds the first instances of every earlier level to the scoring set,
and a confirmation step reruns the champion on fresh seeds. Solutions are finally checked outside the search on held-out sizes, adversarial
inputs and exhaustive small inputs.

\subsection{Generic Generators}
Most candidates come from a grammar-based generator augmented with a small set of \emph{generic block generators}. Each draws every part of a
block independently, so that a named algorithm (a sum, a maximum, factorial, a gcd, a primality test) is reachable only as one draw among many and
is not written down as a whole. The catalogue of loop shapes and relations is nonetheless hand-chosen domain knowledge, so ``no learning'' does not mean ``no prior'':
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{combinator}, a product of catalogued axes: the layout of the input (whole array, key-prefixed, length-prefixed, a pair of sequences),
        the loop shape (single, nested, triangular, converging, adjacent pairs) and the relation computed in the innermost statement (sum, best,
        count-if, exists, for-all, xor, swap-if, and stateful ones: Kadane-style scans, prefix and suffix combinations, longest increasing subsequence, frequency tables);
  \item a \emph{scalar-loop} generator for inputs that are numbers rather than vectors (counting loops over a bound, pair updates until zero);
  \item enumeration of the short tail that follows a generated block, and a mutation that rewrites a single condition in place.
\end{itemize}
Section~\ref{sec:results} concerns seven further pieces added to this set during the work reported here. Each is a probability of replacing one
choice inside a block, and a probability of zero removes it without drawing a random number, so the old grammar is recovered exactly:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{term expression}: the loop-sum term \texttt{arr[i]} becomes a short arithmetic expression of it (\texttt{x/c}, \texttt{x\%c}, \texttt{x*c}, \texttt{x$\pm$c}, \texttt{x/c$_1\pm$c$_2$}, \texttt{x*c$_1\pm$c$_2$}),
  \item \emph{prefix crossing}: the first index at which a running sum crosses a threshold,
  \item \emph{pair find}: the first pair of elements whose sum or difference reaches a target, with two outputs,
  \item \emph{parity term}: a term that depends on the parity of the index, with an optional fold of values above a constant,
  \item \emph{sum-if}: a conditional sum, \texttt{if (a CMP rhs) acc += a},
  \item \emph{radix}: a mixed-radix decomposition of a number with one to three small divisors,
  \item \emph{count-mod}: a count of the elements whose remainder by a small constant is, or is not, zero.
\end{itemize}
The first six were designed after observing where the search failed on the integer subset of PSB2, and several are close to the solution of the problem that motivated them (prefix crossing and Basement, pair find and Find Pair, term expression and Fuel Cost, parity term and Luhn); the last after a failure on a counting problem (Section~\ref{sec:narrow}).

\subsection{Hand-written Skeletons}
\label{sec:skeletons}
A small library of whole-algorithm skeletons (selection sort, heap sort, binary search, merge sort and two travelling-salesman searches) remains
available to seed candidates. Earlier ablations in the project log found them indispensable only for the sorting and search problems of our own suite. We did not run a
skeleton ablation on the PSB2 problems; the solved programs we read for them are plain loops of the kind the generic generators produce.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Benchmark protocol.}
We follow the PSB2 protocol~\cite{helmuth2021psb2} for the problems it covers. A run receives 200 training cases, composed of every
hand-chosen edge case of the official dataset followed by random cases up to 200, and is judged on 2,000 unseen random cases from
the same dataset. A run succeeds only if the returned program passes every training case and all 2,000 unseen cases. The seed
selects the sample of cases, so a different seed is a different training set as well as a different search. The recommended budget of 60 million program
executions is enforced as a cap, together with a wall-clock limit (900\,s unless stated). The dataset files are those published with
PSB2; because the random file has a million lines, the first 50,000 are used. Our reference implementation of each problem is checked against
every loaded official case, and a disagreement is reported; the references of the seven integer problems agree with all of them.

\paragraph{Problems and encodings.}
The language reads an integer array \texttt{arr} (its length is a variable) and writes the answer at its front. The seven PSB2 problems
that need only integers and vectors of integers are used as they are: Fuel Cost, Basement, GCD, Find Pair, Coin Sums, Luhn and Paired
Digits. Other problems (Cut Vector, Fizz Buzz, Mastermind, Twitter, Middle Character, Solve Boolean, Leaders) were given
\emph{lossless encodings that we designed}, for example a string as its character codes and a two-way answer as a negative code; these are
not the official formats and the results on them are reported separately as a transfer test. A further set of 18 problems in the style of
PSB1 (counting, prefix and digit problems) was generated by our own code from the problem descriptions, with input ranges chosen by us;
they too are not an official dataset. The problems that need floating point or free-form strings are not supported.

\paragraph{Search configuration.}
Unless a table says otherwise: beam width 500, 1,500 candidates per size, programs of at most 150 nodes, a step limit of 20,000
interpreter steps per case, seven integer variables, one thread per run. The defaults of the generic pieces are those of the current
version of the system; an ablation arm sets the probability of each of the six pieces of Section~\ref{sec:search} to zero in the same binary,
so that the two arms differ only in those flags.

\paragraph{Matched seeds and tests.}
Every comparison between two configurations uses the same seeds. Paired comparisons (the same seed in two arms) use an exact McNemar test and unpaired ones a two-sided Fisher exact test. The tests are not corrected for multiple comparisons, so the few p-values below are descriptive. Wilson intervals are tabulated only for Table~\ref{tab:psb2}; for reference, 28 of 30 has a 95\% Wilson interval of about 79--98\% and 91 of 100 one of about 84--95\%. Seed ranges used for different questions are disjoint, with the exceptions listed below
(70000--70029 for the first PSB2 runs and 80000--80099 for the 100-seed runs; the transfer tests use other problems, and, apart from the first
PSB2 transfer test, other ranges), so that a result is not re-tested on the seeds that
motivated it. The reuse is intentional in three places: the budget experiments rerun the same 100 seeds, the random-search control and the held-out test use the first 30 of them (80000--80029) so that its arms are paired with the beam arms, and the held-out problems are different from those of the 100-seed run.

\paragraph{Predictions registered before the runs.}
For each experiment after the first, we wrote the expected outcome in the project log before launching it, with numerical bounds,
and recorded the result against it whatever it was. The log is a file in the repository that we control, so the ordering is checkable only through the commit history, not through an external registry. Of the experiments in the log, several predictions failed: the headline of ``six of seven
problems above 93\%'' (30 seeds) did not hold at 100 seeds; a diagnosis that a block of generated code caused the Fuel Cost failures was
refuted by an ablation; and a piece designed to count items by a term of the element did not reach a sibling problem at the registered probability.
These are reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} and not omitted.

\paragraph{Regression battery and promotion rule.}
A change becomes a default only if it passes a regression battery of 77 problems of our suite (sorting, selection, graph, counting and sequence
problems with small instances), each run with 10 matched seeds under a per-problem wall limit of 180 to 240\,s. A problem counts as solved in a
seed if the search keeps at least one program that also passes an independent check outside the search (a benchmark of unseen inputs,
an adversarial set and exhaustive small inputs). The rule is that no problem loses two or more of its 10 seeds and the aggregate is not
worse. The aggregate count of the battery is noisy, about $\pm 3$ between repeats of the same configuration, because runs are limited by wall time.

\paragraph{Hardware and reproducibility.}
All runs were made on one laptop (Intel Core i5-8250U, four cores and eight hardware threads, 16\,GB), eight runs in parallel, compiled
with \texttt{g++} 13 at \texttt{-O3}. Because the wall-clock limit is shared with other load, the depth a run reaches at a given time is not
reproducible to the second: ten failed Luhn seeds were rerun at 1,800\,s and three of the eight that succeeded did so within 900\,s, where
they had failed before. The cap on program executions is deterministic and is the criterion that PSB2 defines, but a stricter reproduction would use only it.
The log with the commands, seeds and raw counts of every experiment is part of the repository (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\paragraph{Cost of the search.}
Counting the candidates scored per run (median over the 100 runs; one candidate is scored on 200 training cases), GCD, Basement and Find Pair are solved in the first size step, after about 1,500 candidates; Paired Digits needs about 13,000; Luhn and Fuel Cost about 75,000 and 94,000; Coin Sums about 302,000, most of them runs that end on the 60 million cap. Scoring takes 98--99\% of wall time in every problem (generation under 2\%), so the wall limit measures evaluations. The number of executions to a solve is the more portable cost: medians of 0.3 million (GCD, Basement, Find Pair), 2.8 (Paired Digits), 13.9 (Luhn) and 16.0 (Fuel Cost).

\paragraph{Cost against a PushGP run.}
We could not rerun the PushGP baseline. We installed the reference implementation of the PSB2 paper (Clojush, tag \texttt{psb2-v1.0}, with its published settings: 1,000 individuals, 300 generations, lexicase selection and UMAD) and ran Fuel Cost on the same laptop, using all eight hardware threads. A generation took about 4.5 minutes with the default JVM and about 3.1 minutes with a tuned heap and parallel collector, taken from the first five and the first three generations, after which we stopped each run. One generation is 200 thousand executions (1,000 individuals on 200 cases), so the throughput is roughly 800 to 1,000 executions per second on eight threads, against a median of 64 thousand per second for one of our Fuel Cost runs on one thread (eight runs in parallel). A run that does not solve would take 300 generations, between 16 and 22 hours; our median solved Fuel Cost run takes 4 minutes, the slowest 15. This is a measurement of one problem, one machine, a few early generations and a reference implementation not tuned for speed, in a different language; it says how long the two programs take for the same 60 million executions, not that our search needs fewer of them (Fuel Cost: median 16 million to solve, against a cap of 60). It is also the reason there is no rerun of the baseline here: 210 runs would take months on this machine.

\subsection{Ablation of the Pieces}
\label{sec:ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} gives the effect of the six pieces designed on the integer subset, with 30 seeds per problem (seeds 70000--70029, 900\,s). These are the seeds on which the pieces and their probabilities were developed, so the table is in-sample. We therefore repeated the ablation on the 100 final seeds (80000--80099, Table~\ref{tab:psb2}, column ``Pieces off''): with the six pieces off the search solves 113 of 700 runs (GCD 100, Paired Digits 13, nothing else) against 595 with them on. Every prediction registered for this run held. No seed solved with the pieces off was missed with them on, and the paired difference is significant for each problem except GCD (exact McNemar, $p<10^{-4}$). The effect is the same size as in the in-sample table, so the seeds are not the explanation; it also means that the pieces account for essentially all the gain over the base search on these problems.

\paragraph{Is the beam needed? A random-search control.}
To separate the beam's selection from the generators, we ran a baseline that keeps the generators but removes all feedback: at each size only fresh random programs are scored (2,000 per size, so the 150 sizes spend the 60 million executions), with no growth or mutation of survivors, no tail enumeration and no crossover. Table~\ref{tab:arms} gives the four arms on the first 30 final seeds (80000--80029), with the cap, not the clock, binding. With the pieces on, random search solves 187 of 210 runs against 183 for the beam; the paired differences are not significant (Luhn 30 against 27, exact McNemar $p=0.25$; Coin Sums 7 against 6, $p=1$), and it needs fewer executions per solved run (median 9.2 against 14.0 million for Fuel Cost). With the pieces off, random search solves GCD only (30 of 210), against 37 for the beam. We had predicted random search to fall well below the beam; six of the seven predictions for it failed. The solved programs of the random arm are single draws of the piece's block (for Fuel Cost, the sum of \texttt{arr[i]/3 - 2}). On these seven problems, then, what the search needs is the generators, and the selection by solved cases adds nothing we can measure. This does not show that selection never matters: ``random'' here means fresh draws from the same hand-built generators, not uniform sampling of syntax trees, and the evidence that selection is needed comes from the sorting and search problems of our suite and, in Section~\ref{sec:heldout}, from Median.

\paragraph{Cost on the rest of the suite.}
A piece that helps the seven problems is only acceptable if it does not hurt the others, so each change was run through the
77-problem regression battery of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. Term expression and prefix crossing together solved 755 of 770 problem--seed
pairs, the same count as the base. The parity term and pair find at a high probability also gave 755 against 755, but
Elements Twice fell from 10 to 8 of 10 seeds, which breaks the rule. A 30-seed repeat gave 29 of 30 at the base, 24 of 30 at the high probability (Fisher exact, two-sided $p \approx 0.10$, not significant)
and 29 of 30 at a low probability, so the probabilities were reduced, as a precaution and not on a significant difference, (parity term 0.03, pair find 0.05, sum-if 0.05, radix 0.03) and the four were
rerun together: 759 against 755 with no drop above one. We do not read the difference of four as a gain, because repeating the base gives a
count that varies by about three. The same noise and the near-ceiling base (755 of 770) limit what the battery can detect: it excludes a loss of two or more seeds in one problem, not a small average cost. The low probabilities still produced the 178 of Table~\ref{tab:ablation}; the high ones had cost a problem.

\subsection{Transfer to Unseen Problems}
\label{sec:transfer}
The pieces were designed while looking at the failures on seven problems, so their effect on those problems may be fitting to the evaluation set. We
tested them on problems that none of them had been designed for, choosing the problems before running either arm, with the same
binary run with the six pieces on (the defaults) and off (all at zero), seeds matched (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). Table~\ref{tab:transfer} gives the
result for 17 problems in three groups: seven further PSB2 problems with our own encodings, and ten PSB1-style problems at
two sizes (the second, medium size is a re-run of the same ten problems with smaller ranges, so the last two rows are not independent evidence).

In no group do we see a difference larger than noise, but the table has little power: Cut Vector is degenerate, Fizz Buzz and Mastermind are at zero in both arms, and in the PSB1-style groups most problems are at zero or at 20 of 20 in both arms, so only a handful of problems can show an effect, and only a large one. The largest per-problem difference is 2 runs (Middle Character 2 against 0 of 20, Fisher exact, two-sided $p \approx 0.49$;
Negative-to-Zero 8 against 10 of 20). Several problems were out of reach for both arms: eight of the ten PSB1-style problems at full
size stayed at zero, which is why we added the medium size, a choice made after seeing that result and not from any difference between the arms, and still six of the
ten stayed at zero. Two of the PSB1-style problems at full size (Grade and Mirror Image) were solved in every run by both arms,
as was the degenerate Cut Vector. Power is the limit. For an unpaired two-sided Fisher test at the 5\% level and 80\% power, 20 runs per arm detect a difference only when it is about 0.3 to 0.45 in success rate (from 0 of 20 the alternative must reach about 7 of 20; from 10 of 20 it must fall to about 2 of 20), and 30 runs detect about 0.25 to 0.35; the largest difference observed in the table is 2 of 20 (0.10), and a problem at zero or at the ceiling in both arms contributes no information about moderate effects. The result thus rules out large effects of the pieces on these problems and says nothing about small or moderate ones. Detecting an effect of 0.15 would take about 140 to 170 runs per arm and problem when the base rate is intermediate (about 50 from a base rate of zero), by the normal approximation.
We conclude only that, for these 17 problems, we detect no effect of the pieces; this is compatible with their effect being confined to the problems they were designed on, but it does not exclude small effects elsewhere.

\subsection{A Frozen Held-Out Test}
\label{sec:heldout}
The transfer problems above were still chosen after the pieces existed, and some were at floor or ceiling. We therefore fixed a test before implementing it: code frozen at the commit of the previous experiments, and four problems that no design or earlier test had used, with predictions written to the log first. They are PSB2 Square Digits, Bowling and Camel Case (official data) and PSB1 Median (three integers in $[-100,100]$). PSB1 distributes generator code, not data files, so the Median cases are produced by a generator we wrote from its description; this is the weakest link of the set. \subsection{A Narrow Piece That Does Transfer Within Its Family}
\label{sec:narrow}
Two problems of the medium PSB1-style set, Count Odds and Sum of Squares, were at zero in both arms. Hand-written programs of about fifteen
statements solved both, so they are expressible. Reading the generator showed two gaps: the combinator's count-if tests the element against a key
and never applies a remainder to it, and the scalar-loop generator has a loop bound of $i^2$ but no term $i^2$ in the body. We closed each with an option.
The first was a wide one: the test drawn from the whole set of arithmetic terms. It reached Count Odds in 1 of 10 runs at probability 0.3, did not reach a sibling problem (Count Multiples of Three, 0 of 20 in both arms) at the registered
probability of 0.1, and, together with the other option, cost one problem of the battery two seeds (Majority Value, 5 to 3 of 10), so it was not adopted.
The narrow option, \emph{count-mod}, tests only \texttt{arr[i] \% c} against zero with $c$ drawn from 2 to 9. At probability 0.1 and with matched seeds,
it raised Count Odds, Count Multiples of Three and Count Evens from 0 of 20 to 18 of 20, and Count Not Multiple of Four to 9 of 20 (Table~\ref{tab:mod}).
A control that the form cannot express (\texttt{x \% 5 == 2}) stayed at 0 of 20. The regression battery gave 758 against 755 with no drop
above one, and the option is now a default. The scalar-loop option reached Sum of Squares in 10 of 10 runs and was left optional.

generator drawing from several dozen forms did not. A possible reading is that the size of the space from which a piece draws matters more than the idea behind it, but the two options were tested at different probabilities, sizes and seed counts, so this is a hypothesis and not a result.

\subsection{Failure Analysis}
\label{sec:failures}
Two problems fall short of 100 of 100 at 900\,s: Fuel Cost (91) and Luhn (90). We asked how much of the gap is budget and how much is the
search missing something, with the predictions registered before each run.

\paragraph{Budget.} We reran both problems on the same 100 seeds with a wall budget of 1800\,s and an execution cap of 120 million (twice the PSB2
cap, so the wall clock stays the binding limit). We predicted at least 96 solved for Fuel Cost and 95 for Luhn. Luhn reached 98 and met the
prediction; Fuel Cost reached 94 and missed it. Inside the first 900\,s the rerun solved 90 and 91 runs, against 91 and 90 originally, so
the timing noise of wall-limited runs is about one run. Eleven of the 19 runs that failed at 900\,s now solve (Fuel Cost 3 of 9, Luhn 8 of 10),
and none that solved before failed. The missing tenth of Luhn was therefore almost entirely budget, and its two remaining failures ended one at the wall limit and one by exhausting the search. The medians among solved runs are 264\,s (Fuel Cost) and 302\,s (Luhn), with a tail: 4 and 7 runs finish after 900\,s.
The wall limit and the doubled cap depart from our 900\,s protocol, but no solved run used more than 60.0 million executions (maxima 59,989,000 for Fuel Cost and 56,441,400 for Luhn, counting one execution per candidate and training case), so 94 and 98 are what the PSB2 cap alone would have allowed if wall time had not bound. At 900\,s the wall limit ended all ten Luhn failures and seven of nine Fuel Cost failures; the other two Fuel Cost failures and 85 of the 86 Coin Sums failures ended on the 60 million cap.

\paragraph{Fuel Cost is different.} Six Fuel Cost runs stopped between 544 and 1225\,s, before the 1800\,s wall budget. The logs show they scored 0.3--0.4 million candidates (below the 120 million cap, at most 75 million executions) and ended by exhausting the program-size limit of 150 nodes, with the best program stuck at 48 of 200 training cases since size 70. More wall time or a higher cap would not help them. We predicted that a larger size limit would not help either (at most one of six solved); all six solved with a limit of 300 nodes, at 1,315--1,545\,s and 79--107 million executions. So the plateau was not final, but the fix costs more than the 60 million executions of the PSB2 cap, and under that criterion these six runs remain failures. The programs found are small (28--33 nodes in five of the six), so the limit is not about room, and why a higher limit changes the outcome is not examined here. A first diagnosis blamed a
block of generated code for the Fuel Cost failures (\S\ref{sec:protocol}); the evidence did not support it. We then tested the opposite
hypothesis, that the parity term was drawing the search into a local attractor: with the parity term disabled, Fuel Cost fell from 91 to 81 of
100 (exact McNemar test on the paired seeds, $p=0.041$), against the prediction of at least 95. The parity term helps on Fuel Cost, so the
attractor hypothesis is rejected, and what holds the last runs back is the size limit and the cost of passing it, not a missing piece.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.}
On the integer subset of PSB2 and under the PSB2 protocol, a search with no learning solves four of seven problems in every one of 100 runs, two in about 90 and one
in 14, rates above the published PushGP counts on all seven, which are a historical reference and not a controlled comparison (Section~\ref{sec:psb2}). The six generic pieces together account for the difference from our own
baseline (their individual contributions were measured stepwise on the development seeds, not by leaving one out on the final seeds), with no cost detected on 77 other problems (a battery near its ceiling, with run-to-run noise of about three) (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). A random-search control with the same generators matches the beam (Table~\ref{tab:arms}), so the result belongs to the generators and not to the selection. A single narrow piece closes a family of counting problems that the previous
grammar did not reach (Section~\ref{sec:narrow}).

\paragraph{What it does not support.}
The central caveat is that the six pieces that lift the PSB2 results were designed after observing where the search failed on \emph{those} problems. Their effect on
them is therefore not a held-out result. The transfer experiments of Section~\ref{sec:transfer} were designed to test that, and detected no effect on 17 other problems, and a frozen held-out set of four (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}) shows none either, although all four of its problems are at floor or ceiling in both arms. In the earlier set some problems were
degenerate or out of reach for both arms, and the test had power only for large effects (about 0.3 to 0.45 in success rate at 20 runs per arm). We read this as a limit on the claim, not as a failure of the pieces: a piece can be useful
exactly on the structure it was built for, and that is also what the count-mod result shows. But it means that the PSB2 numbers describe this system
with its pieces tuned to these seven problems and must not be read as the expected performance on a new problem. Nor do they show that beam search is a good way to find programs: with the generators fixed, random search does as well on these seven problems, although on Median, where no piece applies, it finds nothing and the beam solves every run.

\paragraph{Engineering of the search space or discovery of algorithms.}
Table~\ref{tab:pieces} lists, for each piece, the problem whose failure motivated it. Each piece makes one family of programs likely enough to be drawn; for four of the six, that family is close to the target of the problem that motivated it. What the experiments measure is therefore how much a hand-built bias is worth, not whether the search discovers the structure; the random-search control of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} makes this concrete, since sampling from the generators without any selection does as well as the beam on all seven problems. A search that must find such pieces for itself, for instance by learning a library as DreamCoder does, would answer a different question, and ours leaves it open. We would call the system program synthesis with a hand-built domain bias, and the useful general lesson to be that this bias can be decisive and cheap to isolate and measure.

\paragraph{Coverage of the benchmark.}
Only seven of the 25 PSB2 problems are expressible without floating point or free-form strings. On problems outside that subset, where we built our own encodings, PushGP
solves more than we do (Middle Character 57 of 100 against at most 2 of 20 for us, Fizz Buzz 25 against 0 of 30). The comparison in the first paragraph is thus a
comparison on the subset of the benchmark that suits this language, and we make no claim about the benchmark as a whole. Adding floating point and strings would enlarge the space in ways we have not measured: a float needs a tolerance in place of exact equality and an operator set that does not close over the integers, and a string needs a representation, an index space and a character alphabet, each multiplying the choices of every generated block. We expect the generic generators to need new relations (for example, split and join) and the beam to need a different tie-break, since partial credit by position, as for sorting, does not apply.

\paragraph{Comparison with the published baseline.}
The PushGP figures are those of one untuned configuration reported with the benchmark. Our figures use a wall-clock limit as well as the cap on program
executions, our own tuning of generic pieces, and a single configuration with no tuned comparison arm. A fair comparison would run a tuned PushGP, or another
non-neural system, at the same budget on the same seeds, which we did not do: the reference implementation proved too slow to rerun on our hardware (Section~\ref{sec:psb2}). GCD is also a problem that the search was built to solve from its earliest versions, so it is not independent evidence.

\paragraph{Budget and reproducibility.}
The success rates of Fuel Cost and Luhn depend on the time limit: their curves are still rising at 900\,s and a rerun of failed Luhn seeds at twice the time solved most of them. Because the limit is on wall-clock time, results
carry timing noise that depends on machine load, and are not reproducible to the second. The cap on program executions is deterministic and a stricter
reproduction would use only it. The results come from one machine.

\paragraph{Our own problem sets.}
Four groups of problems are ours: the Median data of the held-out test, generated by our own code from the PSB1 description; lossless encodings of PSB2 problems that are not in the official format, PSB1-style problems generated by our own code with ranges chosen by us (and
changed once after seeing the first result, which is a forking path that we report), and families of siblings written to test a piece. These are useful for
testing mechanisms but are not external benchmarks.

\paragraph{Process.}
The work was done with an AI coding assistant, which wrote much of the code and the analysis scripts; design decisions, the predictions and the reading of results were reviewed by the
author. We state this because it affects how the process, and not the numbers, should be assessed, and the full log is available for that.

\section{Conclusion}
A beam search over a small imperative language, with no learning, solves the seven integer problems of PSB2 at rates between 14 and 100 of 100
runs, and four of them in every run. The supported claim is narrow: those rates, on those problems, under the PSB2 success criterion. The
pieces that produced them were designed on the same problems, and on 17 unseen problems we detect no effect of them; the one
piece that transferred did so within a family of closely related problems. A held-out set frozen before implementation is consistent with the first point: the pieces make no difference on four new problems, but one (Median) is solved by the base grammar in every run and three stay out of reach, so it cannot show moderate effects. A control that keeps the generators and removes the selection
(random search) solves as many runs as the beam, so on those problems the result belongs to the hand-built bias and not to the search; on Median, where no piece applies, random search fails and the beam does not. Failed predictions, including a wrong diagnosis of the Fuel
Cost failures and the expectation that random search would fall well below the beam, are part of the record. The next steps are a tuned baseline arm on the same 100 seeds, PSB2 problems that need strings and
floats (which the language cannot yet express), and a second held-out set that includes problems with strings and state machines.
