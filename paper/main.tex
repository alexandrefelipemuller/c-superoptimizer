\section{Introduction}

Program synthesis from input--output examples asks for a program, not a function approximation: the result can be read, tested
outside the examples, and reasoned about. The field has two long-standing non-neural lines, genetic programming (GP) and
enumerative or constraint-based synthesis, and, more recently, large neural models that write code directly. The first two have a
shared yardstick in the General Program Synthesis Benchmark Suite and its successor PSB2~\cite{helmuth2015psb1,helmuth2021psb2},
whose protocol (200 training cases, 2,000 unseen test cases, a budget of 60 million program executions, 100 runs per problem) makes
success rates comparable across methods. The published baseline there is PushGP with lexicase selection, which solves 13 of the 25
PSB2 problems at least once and several of the easiest ones only in a minority of runs~\cite{helmuth2021psb2}.

This paper reports a program-synthesis system built to see how far a search with \emph{no learning} can go on that yardstick, and,
as importantly, how much of what it achieves is due to the search itself and how much to pieces that its author designed after
looking at where it failed. The system is a beam search over a small imperative language (integer variables, an array, loops,
conditionals, swaps, bounded recursion) in which candidate programs come from a mix of mutation, fresh generation and a small
set of generic generators (a ``combinator'' of loop shapes and relations, a scalar-loop generator, enumeration of short tails),
with selection by the exact number of solved cases. There is no neural model, no learned prior and no language model.

We emphasise method as much as numbers. Every comparison is run with matched random seeds. Predictions are written down before
the runs and failed predictions are kept in the record. A change becomes a default only if it passes a regression battery of 77
suite problems with a no-drop-of-two rule. The full log (140 numbered sections) is part of the artefact.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item On the integer subset of PSB2 (seven of the 25 problems) and with 100 fresh seeds, the system solves four problems in all
        100 runs, two (Fuel Cost, Luhn) in about 90, and one (Coin Sums) in 14. Under the same protocol the published PushGP baseline
        solves the same seven problems in 8, 50, 1, 4, 0, 8 and 2 of 100 runs (Section~\ref{sec:psb2}). The budget matters: eight of
        ten failed Luhn runs succeed when given twice the time.
  \item An ablation showing where the gain comes from: six generic pieces, each cheap and each tested for cost on the 77-problem suite,
        lift the seven problems from 34 to 178 solved runs out of 210 (30 seeds).
  \item A \emph{negative} transfer result that we regard as the main finding: the pieces were designed on those seven problems, and
        on 17 problems they were not designed for (PSB2 and PSB1-style) they show no gain beyond noise. One narrow, generic form
        (counting items by a remainder test) does close its own family, from 0 to 63 of 80 runs, at no measured cost
        (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}).
  \item A documented protocol and the data behind every claim, including the claims that did not survive (for instance, ``six of
        seven problems above 93\%'' was true at 30 seeds and false at 100).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.}
Only seven of the 25 PSB2 problems are expressible in our language, the integer and vector-of-integer ones; the rest need floating
point or free-form strings. We do not claim to cover PSB2, and on problems that need an encoding outside that subset the system is
not competitive (for example, Middle Character is solved by PushGP in 57 of 100 runs and by our system in at most 2 of 20 with our
own encoding). Section~\ref{sec:discussion} lists the limitations.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} places the work among GP, enumerative and learned
synthesis. Section~\ref{sec:search} describes the search, Section~\ref{sec:protocol} the protocol, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results,
and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} the limitations.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Genetic programming and benchmark suites.}
Push is a stack-based language designed so that evolved programs are always syntactically valid, and PushGP evolves such programs
with a population-based search~\cite{spector2002push}. The General Program Synthesis Benchmark Suite (PSB1) collected 29 problems from
introductory programming sources and gave PushGP results as a reference~\cite{helmuth2015psb1}; PSB2 added 25 harder problems taken
from programming katas and courses, again with a PushGP baseline~\cite{helmuth2021psb2}. Our experiments use the PSB2 protocol and its
data files for the integer subset, and the published PushGP rates as the reference point. We differ in kind, not only in
details: our programs are written in an imperative language with explicit loops and the search is a beam search over generated
candidates, not an evolving population.

\paragraph{Selection, generalisation and novelty.}
Lexicase selection, which filters the population case by case in random order, is the standard parent-selection method for PushGP
on these suites~\cite{helmuth2015lexicase}. It also has known weaknesses: on problems with few distinct outputs, such as Boolean ones,
it overfits the training cases, and generalisation improves when the run continues after the first program that fits the training
set and the smallest such program is chosen~\cite{sobania2021generalizability}. Our protocol keeps a post-solution window and an
independent adversarial check for the same reason. Combining lexicase selection with novelty search was studied by Jundt and
Helmuth~\cite{jundt2019novelty}, who found novelty alone to be of limited use for program synthesis; a behaviour-profile novelty term in our
system gave the same kind of result.

\paragraph{Search for efficient algorithms and recursion.}
GP has been used to evolve list-search algorithms, including logarithmic-time search, by Wolfson and Sipper, who generalise the representation
with automatically defined functions so that the result is not biased towards binary search~\cite{wolfson2010efficient}. This is prior art for
our own experiments on searching for sub-linear algorithms (not reported here), which we do not present as novel.

\paragraph{Enumerative, syntax-guided and sketch-based synthesis.}
Syntax-guided synthesis casts the problem as finding an expression, from a grammar, that meets a specification~\cite{alur2013sygus}, and
enumerative solvers explore that grammar bottom-up, often with equivalence pruning~\cite{gulwani2017program}. Recursive programs can be
synthesised from examples by an enumerative method as well~\cite{albarghouthi2013escher}. These systems mostly work on loop-free
expressions or on a specification given as a formula, while ours searches imperative programs with loops from examples only. Our
short-tail enumeration is a small instance of the same idea: after a block of generated code, the remaining few statements are enumerated.
Stochastic superoptimisation searches over instruction sequences with a cost function~\cite{schkufza2013stoke}; our selection uses
solved-case counts, not a cost model.

\paragraph{Library learning and learned guidance.}
DreamCoder and Stitch learn a library of reusable functions from solved tasks and use it to guide search~\cite{ellis2020dreamcoder,bowers2023stitch}.
Our generic pieces play a similar role, a vocabulary of useful loop shapes, but they are \emph{designed by hand}, not learned, which is both the
reason they transfer poorly to unseen problems and the reason their effect can be isolated and measured.

\paragraph{Language-model-based synthesis.}
Language models trained on code solve many programming problems directly~\cite{chen2021codex}. We do not compare with them: the goal here is a
non-neural baseline with an inspectable search, and the comparison that matters for the claims in this paper is against GP on the same protocol.

\paragraph{Position.}
Our system is not new as a search method; its relation to the above is that it applies a different, imperative-program beam search to a
shared benchmark, reports the failures and negative transfer alongside the successes, and publishes the whole log. The closest results are the
published PushGP rates on PSB2~\cite{helmuth2021psb2}.
